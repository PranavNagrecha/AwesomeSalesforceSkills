# Gotchas — Shield Platform Encryption: BYOK / KMS Setup

Non-obvious behaviours of customer-supplied and customer-held tenant secrets.

## Gotcha 1: You Can Upload Key Material Once Every 24 Hours in Production

**What happens:** A rotation goes wrong — wrong certificate, wrong wrapping mode,
truncated Base64 — and the obvious response is to fix it and upload again. The
second upload is rejected.

> "Customer-supplied key material can be uploaded once every 24 hours in production
> and Developer Edition orgs, and every 4 hours in sandbox orgs."
> — Salesforce Help, *How Shield Platform Encryption Works*

**When it occurs:** On the first production rotation, which is also the first time
the process is executed outside a rehearsal.

**How to avoid:** Rehearse the entire wrap-and-upload in a sandbox, where the window
is four hours, until it succeeds twice consecutively without hand-editing. Then
execute in production at the *start* of a maintenance window, not the end, so a
24-hour cooldown does not overlap a business day. Script the wrapping — a
hand-executed `openssl` pipeline is where the truncation happens.

## Gotcha 2: Rotation Encrypts New Writes Only

**What happens:** A tenant secret is rotated for compliance, the change is signed
off, and an auditor later establishes that most data is still encrypted under the
previous secret.

> "When customer-supplied tenant secrets are uploaded, subsequent data is encrypted
> either with the key derived from the current primary secret and the new
> customer-supplied tenant secret or the new DEK alone."

*Subsequent* data. Existing records stay under the previous secret until they are
explicitly re-encrypted.

**When it occurs:** Every rotation, because the upload succeeds and produces a
visible confirmation, while the re-encryption is a separate job that produces
nothing unless started.

**How to avoid:** Make re-encryption a distinct, separately signed-off step in the
rotation runbook with its own completion check, not a sentence at the end of the
upload step. Verify against the Encryption Statistics page rather than against the
key management screen — the latter tells you which secret is *active*, not which
secret your data is under. Note that re-encryption on a large object is a long
background job, so the rotation window is the job's duration, not the upload's.

## Gotcha 3: Cache-Only Keys Are Not a Callout Per Decryption — They Are Worse and Better

**What happens:** Teams reject Cache-Only Keys on a latency argument that is wrong,
or adopt them believing the cache makes the key service non-critical. Both readings
miss the actual behaviour.

> "After the cache is flushed, the Cache-Only Key Service fetches key material from
> your specified key service. The cache is regularly flushed every 72 hours. Certain
> Salesforce operations flush the cache on average every 24 hours."

- **Better than feared:** steady-state decryption does not call out. Per-operation
  latency is unaffected.
- **Worse than hoped:** the flush schedule is Salesforce's, not yours, and "certain
  Salesforce operations" flush on average every 24 hours. Your key service must be
  available at a moment you cannot predict.

**When it occurs:** The gap between adoption and the first unplanned flush during a
key service outage is often months, so the dependency is invisible until it is not.

**How to avoid:** Frame the SLO correctly. The requirement is not throughput, it is
*availability at an arbitrary instant*, which means multi-region, health-checked,
and monitored independently of Salesforce. Alert on the key service directly — a
service that is up but answering incorrectly produces the same user-visible symptom
as one that is down, and Salesforce's error will not distinguish them.

## Gotcha 4: Replay Detection Requires Your Key Service to Participate

**What happens:** Replay detection is enabled as a hardening step and every callout
begins to fail. The key service was working perfectly a moment earlier.

> "When enabled, replay detection inserts an autogenerated, unique marker called a
> `RequestIdentifier` into every callout, which includes the key identifier, a nonce
> generated for that callout instance, and the nonce required from the endpoint."

The key service must handle the nonce and return what is required. A service that
returns valid key material while ignoring the marker fails once detection is on.

**When it occurs:** During a hardening pass, typically months after the integration
was built and by someone who did not build it.

**How to avoid:** Implement and test the replay-detection path when the key service
is first written, even if detection is left off initially. Then enabling it is a
configuration change rather than a code change under time pressure. Rehearse the
enablement in a sandbox and confirm callouts still succeed before touching
production.

## Gotcha 5: Destroying a Tenant Secret Is Not Reversible, and That Is the Point

**What happens:** A destroy is executed to prove the control works, or as an
over-cautious incident response, and the data encrypted under that secret is
permanently unreadable.

**When it occurs:** During an audit demonstration performed in the wrong org, and
during incident response by someone who has read that destruction is a documented
capability and has not read that it is final.

**How to avoid:** Two rules, both in the runbook.

1. **Destruction is rehearsed only in a sandbox**, quarterly, with the recovery
   steps written down. Capture what the failure actually looks like — the UI error,
   the Apex exception type, the integration's HTTP response — because that is what
   your support desk will need if it ever happens in production.
2. **Production destruction requires a second approver** and an explicit statement
   that the data under that secret is being deliberately abandoned. It is a data
   destruction event, not a security setting change, and should be governed like one.

## Gotcha 6: The Key Model Does Not Change Who Can Read the Field

**What happens:** BYOK is deployed and "customer PII is protected by
customer-managed keys" is recorded as an access control. Nobody's visibility
changed.

Shield Platform Encryption is transparent above the storage layer. Every user with
field-level security Read sees exactly the same plaintext after encryption as
before, under every key model. The key model changes who controls decryption *at the
database tier* — it protects against a storage compromise and against Salesforce
itself, and against nothing a logged-in user does.

**When it occurs:** At the compliance review after go-live, or at the breach. The
rollout "succeeded," so nothing signals that the intended need-to-know restriction
was never implemented.

**How to avoid:** Keep key management and access control as separate workstreams
with separate deliverables. The key model answers "who can decrypt the database."
An FLS matrix answers "who can see the value." A design document that cites BYOK in
its access-control section has conflated two different controls, and the second one
does not exist.

## Gotcha 7: Choosing Cache-Only Without a Contractual Requirement

**What happens:** An architect selects Cache-Only Keys because it is the strongest
option available, and the org inherits a runtime dependency on a key service it
cannot operate to the required standard. The first extended key service outage makes
encrypted fields unreadable across the org.

**When it occurs:** In the design phase, when "strongest available" is easier to
defend in a review than "adequate for the stated requirement."

**How to avoid:** Require the compliance clause to be cited before the model is
chosen, in writing:

```text
Model chosen:  Cache-Only
Requirement:   <the specific clause mandating non-persistence of key material
                in the vendor's systems, or unilateral immediate revocation>
Key service:   Availability SLO, owner, on-call rotation, multi-region design
Fallback:      Rehearsed path back to BYOK or Salesforce-managed
```

If the requirement cannot be cited, BYOK provides key provenance and
crypto-shredding with no runtime availability dependency, and is the right answer
for most orgs that reach for Cache-Only.

## Gotcha 8: Encryption Is Not Retroactive, Whatever the Key Model

**What happens:** An encryption policy is enabled, a compliance audit is passed, and
historical records are still stored in plaintext at the database tier.

Enabling a policy encrypts data written *after* activation. Existing records remain
as they were until a re-encryption job is explicitly run and completes.

**When it occurs:** In every retroactive Shield rollout — that is, every production
org adding Shield after go-live, which is the common case.

**How to avoid:** Make re-encryption job completion a gating criterion for
compliance sign-off, not a follow-up task. Verify on the Encryption Statistics page
per object rather than trusting the policy screen. Budget wall-clock time
proportional to data volume, and stage the rollout object by object so each object's
re-encryption completes before the next begins.

---

## Gotcha 9: The Setup Permission Is Not the Read Permission

**What happens:** A permission review grants **Manage Encryption Keys** to a group
believing it controls who can see encrypted data, or removes it believing that will
hide data from them.

**Manage Encryption Keys** (with **Customize Application**) governs *administering*
the encryption feature: generating, uploading, rotating, and destroying tenant
secrets. It has no bearing on who can read an encrypted field's value — that is
field-level security, and it is the only lever that changes it.

**When it occurs:** During a permission-set review, where both permissions contain
the word "encryption" and neither name distinguishes administration from access.

**How to avoid:** Treat **Manage Encryption Keys** as one of the most dangerous
permissions in the org — it includes the ability to destroy tenant secrets, which is
irreversible data destruction — and scope it to a named, small group with an
approval trail. Then handle read access entirely separately through FLS. Do not let
the two appear in the same section of a review, because their names invite the
confusion.
